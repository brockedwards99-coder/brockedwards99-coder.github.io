DUI & Impaired Driving Charges in British Columbia: What You Need to Know

A criminal impaired driving charge is far more serious than a roadside prohibition. Here's how these charges work under the Criminal Code, what's at stake, and why early legal advice is so important.

By Edwards & Company · Criminal Defence & Personal Injury, Burnaby BC

"DUI" is the everyday term for impaired driving. In British Columbia, an impaired driving allegation can be dealt with in two very different ways: as a provincial administrative penalty — the Immediate Roadside Prohibition — or as a criminal charge under the Criminal Code of Canada. This article focuses on the criminal side, which carries the most serious and lasting consequences.

Unlike a roadside prohibition, a criminal impaired driving conviction produces a permanent criminal record, a mandatory fine, and a mandatory driving prohibition — with consequences that can reach into your job, your insurance, and your ability to travel. If you are facing a charge, understanding what you are up against is the first step.

What counts as impaired driving?

Canadian impaired driving law is set out primarily in section 320.14 of the Criminal Code. It captures several distinct offences:

  • Impaired operation — operating a vehicle while your ability to do so is impaired to any degree by alcohol, a drug, or a combination.
  • "Over 80" — having a blood-alcohol concentration at or over 80 milligrams of alcohol in 100 millilitres of blood (0.08) within two hours of operating a vehicle.
  • Over the drug limit — having a prohibited blood-drug concentration within two hours of driving. For cannabis (THC), the limit is 5 nanograms per millilitre of blood.
  • Combined alcohol and drug — having both 50 milligrams of alcohol and 2.5 nanograms of THC (or higher) within two hours of driving.

Note that impairment "to any degree" is enough — you do not need to be visibly drunk. And the offences apply to alcohol and drugs, including cannabis and some prescription medications.

The two-hour rule

The "over 80" and over-the-drug-limit offences are measured within two hours of when you stopped operating the vehicle. This is a deliberate feature of the law and it narrows some traditional defences. It's one of several reasons these cases are more technical than they first appear.

You don't have to be driving: "care or control"

A common and costly misunderstanding is that you're safe as long as the car isn't moving. In fact, the law reaches anyone in care or control of a motor vehicle — which in some circumstances can include a person sitting in a stationary vehicle. Whether care or control existed is a fact-specific question, and it is often a live issue in these cases.

Refusing a breath or blood sample

Under section 320.15 of the Criminal Code, it is a separate offence to fail or refuse, without a reasonable excuse, to comply with a lawful demand for a breath or blood sample. Refusal is treated just as seriously as being over the limit — and in fact carries a higher mandatory minimum fine on a first offence. Police in BC also have the power to make mandatory roadside breath demands.

Mandatory penalties on conviction

The Criminal Code sets mandatory minimum penalties for impaired driving. A judge cannot go below them. The minimums escalate with each subsequent offence:

OffenceMinimum penaltyMinimum driving prohibition
First$1,000 fine (higher for elevated BAC; $2,000 for refusal)1 year
Second30 days imprisonment2 years
Third or more120 days imprisonment3 years

On a first offence, the minimum fine itself rises with the blood-alcohol reading, and refusal carries a higher minimum. These are floors, not ceilings — sentences can be greater depending on the circumstances, and cases involving bodily harm or death are treated far more severely, with the potential for lengthy imprisonment.

The consequences beyond the courtroom

The formal sentence is only part of the impact. A criminal impaired driving conviction commonly brings:

  • A permanent criminal record, which can affect employment and volunteer opportunities.
  • Travel difficulties, particularly entry to the United States.
  • Higher insurance costs, including ICBC's Driver Risk Premium.
  • Ignition Interlock requirements to re-license.
  • The Responsible Driver Program or other remedial requirements.
  • Provincial driving prohibitions that can extend well beyond the Criminal Code minimums for repeat convictions.

Over a working lifetime, the true cost of a conviction — in lost opportunities as much as dollars — can be substantial.

Criminal charge or roadside prohibition?

In BC, most drinking-and-driving situations are now handled through the administrative Immediate Roadside Prohibition scheme rather than criminal court. The attending officer effectively decides which route to take. A criminal charge generally follows more serious circumstances, and it typically involves being taken to the station for testing on an approved instrument. Sometimes you can face both an IRP and a criminal charge from the same incident.

Your rights and how a charge proceeds

If you're arrested on a criminal impaired driving allegation, you'll usually be taken to a police station for breath testing on an approved instrument, and your vehicle may be towed. You have the right to speak with a lawyer, and how the police handled the stop, the demand, the testing, and your rights can all become important issues in your defence. Because so much turns on procedure and evidence, what happens in the first hours and days matters.

Frequently asked questions

What is the legal alcohol limit for driving in BC?

Under the Criminal Code, it's an offence to be at or over 80 milligrams of alcohol in 100 millilitres of blood (0.08) within two hours of operating a vehicle. BC also imposes administrative penalties from 0.05 upward through the Immediate Roadside Prohibition scheme.

Can I be charged with DUI if I wasn't driving?

Yes. The offence covers having care or control of a vehicle, not just driving it — in some circumstances that can include sitting in a stationary vehicle. It depends on the specific facts.

What happens if I refuse a breath test?

Refusing a lawful breath or blood demand without a reasonable excuse is itself a criminal offence, treated as seriously as being over the limit, and it carries its own mandatory minimum penalty.

Is a first-offence DUI a criminal record in BC?

A conviction for a Criminal Code impaired driving offence creates a criminal record, along with a minimum fine and a minimum one-year driving prohibition. This is different from an IRP, which is administrative and carries no criminal record.
