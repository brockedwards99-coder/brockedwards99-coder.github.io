Immediate Roadside Prohibitions (IRP) in British Columbia: A Complete Guide

If you've been handed a driving prohibition at the roadside, the clock is already running. Here is what an IRP means, what it costs, and — most importantly — the short window you have to fight it.

By Edwards & Company · Criminal Defence & Personal Injury, Burnaby BC

An Immediate Roadside Prohibition — almost always called an "IRP" — is the most common way impaired driving is dealt with in British Columbia today. It is fast, it is automatic, and it takes effect the moment the officer hands you the notice. It is also one of the few legal consequences in the province with a deadline measured in days, not months.

Because an IRP is issued at the roadside rather than in a courtroom, many drivers assume there is nothing they can do about it. That is not true. There is a formal review process — but the window to use it is extremely short. This guide explains how IRPs work, what penalties apply, and how the review process operates, so you can make an informed decision quickly.

What is an IRP?

An Immediate Roadside Prohibition is an administrative penalty issued by a police officer under British Columbia's Motor Vehicle Act. It is administered by the Superintendent of Motor Vehicles through RoadSafetyBC. Crucially, an IRP is not a criminal charge — receiving one does not, on its own, give you a criminal record.

An officer can require any driver in care or control of a motor vehicle to provide a breath sample into an Approved Screening Device (ASD) — the handheld roadside breathalyzer. What the device registers determines what kind of prohibition, if any, follows.

Warn vs. Fail: the two readings that matter

The device produces one of two results that lead to an IRP:

  • Warn — corresponds to a blood-alcohol concentration in roughly the 0.05 to 0.08 range. A Warn leads to a shorter prohibition that escalates with each repeat within a five-year period.
  • Fail — corresponds to a blood-alcohol concentration of 0.08 or higher, and results in a 90-day prohibition. Refusing to provide a breath sample carries the same consequences as a Fail.

You have the right to request a second sample on a separate device, and to have the lower of the two readings prevail. Whether that right was properly offered is one of the things a lawyer will examine.

IRP penalties at a glance

The consequences escalate with the reading and with any prior prohibitions. The figures below reflect the general framework, but exact monetary penalties and fees are set by regulation and change from time to time — always confirm current amounts with RoadSafetyBC.

A Fail or refusal also stays on your driving record permanently, even though it is not a criminal conviction.

What an IRP really costs

The prohibition itself is only part of the picture. Once towing, storage, program fees, reinstatement, and insurance surcharges are added up, a 90-day IRP frequently costs a driver several thousand dollars. Typical additional costs include:

  • Responsible Driver Program (RDP) — a remedial education program, currently around $930 including tax.
  • Ignition Interlock Program (IIP) — where required, the device runs roughly $1,500 per year, with a minimum term depending on your history.
  • ICBC Driver Risk Premium — an insurance surcharge that can apply for three years following certain prohibitions.
  • Towing and 30-day storage — paid by the driver for impounded vehicles.

Industry estimates commonly place the all-in cost of an IRP between roughly $600 for a short Warn and $4,600 or more for a Fail. If the prohibition is set aside on review, most of these consequences fall away with it — which is why the review process matters so much.

How to challenge an IRP: the review process

You do not go before a judge to fight an IRP. Instead, you apply for a review decided by an adjudicator at RoadSafetyBC. Here is how it works:

Key facts about the review
  • Deadline: apply within 7 calendar days of being served.
  • How to apply: online or in person at an ICBC driver licensing office.
  • Fee: approximately $100 for a written review, or $200 for an oral (telephone) review.
  • Type: a written review is available for all IRPs; an oral review is generally available only for 30-day and 90-day prohibitions.
  • Format: the review is paper-based and decided on the evidence and written submissions — there is no trial.
  • Burden: the burden is on you, the applicant, to show the prohibition should not stand.

Because the burden falls on the driver and the timeline is so tight, the quality of the written submissions and supporting evidence often decides the outcome. This is where experienced representation makes the biggest difference.

Grounds for challenging an IRP

An adjudicator can revoke an IRP where the legal requirements were not met. Common grounds a lawyer will investigate include:

  • Whether the officer had a lawful basis to demand a breath sample.
  • Whether the Approved Screening Device was reliable, properly maintained, and correctly calibrated.
  • Whether your right to a second test on a different device was honoured, with the lower reading used.
  • Whether the device was operated correctly and within the manufacturer's and regulatory requirements.
  • Whether you were actually the person driving or in care and control of the vehicle.
  • Whether the officer followed every procedural step the legislation requires.

Not every case has a viable ground, and a responsible lawyer will tell you honestly whether a challenge is worth pursuing before you commit to the process and the fee.

What if the review is unsuccessful?

A RoadSafetyBC review decision is final at the administrative level. If your review is dismissed, the only further avenue is a judicial review in the Supreme Court of British Columbia, started by filing a Petition. There is a limitation period for judicial review, so once again, timing is critical. A lawyer can advise whether judicial review is realistic in your situation.

IRP vs. a criminal impaired driving charge

Since BC expanded its administrative scheme, most drinking-and-driving situations are now handled through the IRP system rather than criminal court. That is generally to a driver's advantage — no criminal record, and faster resolution. However, the two are not mutually exclusive: you can receive an IRP and a separate Criminal Code charge from the same incident. More serious cases — particularly impaired driving causing bodily harm or death — are still prosecuted criminally, with significant penalties. If you are facing both, you need advice that covers the administrative review and the criminal matter together.

Frequently asked questions

How long do I have to dispute an IRP in BC?

You have 7 calendar days from the date the prohibition was served to apply for a review with RoadSafetyBC. It is a strict deadline; missing it generally forfeits your right to challenge the prohibition.

Is an IRP a criminal charge?

No. An IRP is an administrative penalty under the Motor Vehicle Act, so it does not create a criminal record on its own. It can, however, be issued alongside a separate Criminal Code charge from the same incident.

Can an IRP be cancelled?

Yes. An adjudicator can revoke an IRP on review if the legal requirements were not met — for example, an unreliable screening device, no lawful grounds for the demand, or a denied right to a second test.

What does a Fail reading mean?

A Fail generally corresponds to a blood-alcohol concentration of 0.08 or higher and results in a 90-day prohibition, a 30-day vehicle impoundment, and additional penalties. Refusing to provide a sample carries the same consequences.
